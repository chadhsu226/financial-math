6.1 Expected Return

R̄ = Σi=1n Pi × Ri

The expected return is simply the probability-weighted average of the possible returns across scenarios.

6.2 Quantifying Risk: Variance and Standard Deviation

σ² = Σi=1n Pi × (Ri − R̄)²
σ = √σ²

Variance measures the expected squared deviation of returns from their expected value; standard deviation converts this back into the same percentage units as returns, making it easier to interpret.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

Example 6.2: Calculating Risk (Asset A)

Continuing Example 6.1, with R̄A = 8.5%:

σ²A = 0.3×(0.20−0.085)² + 0.4×(0.10−0.085)² + 0.3×(−0.05−0.085)²
σ²A = 0.009525
σA = √0.009525 ≈ 9.76%

6.3 Portfolio Theory and Diversification

Portfolio Expected Return

R̄P = Σj=1m wj × R̄j

Portfolio Variance (Two-Asset Case)

σ²P = w²Aσ²A + w²Bσ²B + 2wAwBσAσBρAB
FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

The Meaning of the Correlation Coefficient (ρAB)

ρAB = CovAB / (σA × σB)
  • ρAB = +1.0: The two assets move perfectly together; risk cannot be diversified away.
  • ρAB = −1.0: The two assets move perfectly opposite each other; risk can be fully eliminated (at a specific weighting).
  • ρAB = 0: The assets are unrelated, giving a significant diversification effect.
FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

Example of the Diversification Effect

Two assets each with a 50% weight (both with the same distribution as Asset A), with ρAB = 0 (fully independent):

σ²P = 0.5² × 0.009525 + 0.5² × 0.009525 + 0 = 0.0047625
σP = √0.0047625 ≈ 6.90% < σA = 9.76%

While holding the expected return fixed at 8.5%, risk falls from 9.76% to 6.90%, demonstrating the effectiveness of diversification.

Asset A has an expected return of 20% (probability 0.6) and −10% (probability 0.4). What is R̄A?

Show solution

Exercise 2

Continuing Question 1, calculate σ²A and σA.

Show solution
σ²A = 0.6×(20%−8%)² + 0.4×(−10%−8%)² = 0.0216
σA = √0.0216 ≈ 14.70%

Exercise 3

Portfolio P invests 70% in Asset A with R̄A=8% and 30% in Asset B with R̄B=5%. What is R̄P?

Show solution
R̄P = 0.7 × 8% + 0.3 × 5% = 7.10%
FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

Exercise 4

Continuing Question 3, with σA=14.70%, σB=10%, and ρAB=+1.0, what is σP?

Show solution
σ²P = 0.7²×14.70%² + 0.3²×10%² + 2×0.7×0.3×14.70%×10%×1.0 = 1.7649%
σP = √1.7649% ≈ 13.29%

At ρ=+1, σP equals exactly the weighted average of the individual standard deviations (0.7×14.70%+0.3×10%=13.29%), so there is no diversification effect at all.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

Exercise 5

Continuing Question 3, if ρAB=−1.0, what is σP?

Show solution
σ²P = 0.7²×14.70%² + 0.3²×10%² + 2×0.7×0.3×14.70%×10%×(−1.0) = 0.5314%
σP = √0.5314% ≈ 7.29%

The more negative the correlation coefficient, the stronger the diversification effect; risk reduction is greatest when ρ=−1.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 6

Exercise 6

When calculating a portfolio's total risk, which factor measures how closely the two assets' movements are related? If that factor is negative, how does it affect overall risk?

Show solution

The factor that measures the relationship between the two assets' movements is the correlation coefficient ρAB (or the covariance CovAB). When ρAB is negative, it means the two assets' returns tend to move in opposite directions: when one rises, the other tends to fall. This makes the cross term in the portfolio variance formula (2wAwBσAσBρAB) negative, offsetting part of each asset's individual risk. As a result, the overall portfolio risk (standard deviation) is lower than that of holding either asset alone — this is the core principle of diversification.

Chapter Summary

  • Expected return is the probability-weighted average of returns across scenarios; variance and standard deviation measure the extent to which returns deviate from their expected value (risk).
  • Standard deviation is more practical than variance because its units match those of returns, making it easier to interpret and compare directly.
  • A portfolio's risk depends not only on the risk of the individual assets but also on the correlation between them: the lower the correlation (especially as it approaches −1), the stronger the diversification effect.
