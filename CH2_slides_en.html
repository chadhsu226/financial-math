2.1 Fundamentals of cash flow

A cash flow is the actual inflow or outflow of funds at a specific point in time.

  • Inflow (+): funds coming in, represented as a positive value.
  • Outflow (−): funds going out, represented as a negative value.

Accurately identifying the amount and timing of each cash flow is the foundation for all the annuity calculations that follow.

2.2 Definition and classification of annuities

An annuity is a series of consecutive cash flows of equal amount, spaced at equal time intervals.

TypeTiming of paymentCommon examples
Ordinary AnnuityEnd of each periodMonthly mortgage or auto loan payments
Annuity DueBeginning of each periodRent payments; savings deposits made at the start of the month
PerpetuityContinues indefinitelyFixed dividends on some preferred shares

2.3 Future value of annuities: ordinary annuity

FVA = C × [(1 + r)n − 1] / r

The accumulated total after depositing C at the end of each period for n periods, compounded at rate r per period.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 2

Future value of an annuity due

FVAD = C × [(1 + r)n − 1] / r × (1 + r)
FVAD = FVA × (1 + r)

Every cash flow in an annuity due earns one extra period of interest compared with an ordinary annuity, so FVAD is always greater than FVA.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 2

Example 2.2: Computing the future value of an annuity due

Ming deposits C = 10,000 at the beginning of each year, at an annual rate of r = 4%, for n = 8 years.

FVA = 10,000 × [(1.04)8 − 1] / 0.04 ≈ 92,142.26
FVAD = 92,142.26 × 1.04 ≈ 95,827.95

2.4 Present value of annuities: ordinary annuity

PVA = C × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r

The present-day equivalent total of a stream of C per period for n future periods, discounted back to today.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 2

Present value of an annuity due and a perpetuity

PVAD = PVA × (1 + r)
PVP = C / r

The present value of a perpetuity is simply the periodic amount divided by the rate, because the cash flow never ends (as n → ∞, (1+r)−n → 0).

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 2

Example 2.3: Present value of a mortgage (ordinary annuity)

Hua can afford a mortgage payment of C = 25,000 per month. The annual rate is 1.8%, compounded monthly, over a 20-year repayment term.

Monthly rate r = 0.018 / 12 = 0.0015; total number of periods n = 20 × 12 = 240

PVA = 25,000 × [1 − (1.0015)−240] / 0.0015
PVA ≈ 5,035,591.07

In other words, Hua can qualify for a mortgage of approximately 5,035,591.

After retiring, a person withdraws 50,000 at the beginning of each month for 20 years, at an annual rate of 3% (compounded monthly). How much must be deposited now?

Show solution

Comprehensive Exercise 6: Case discussion (Olympic gold medal)

You can take a lump sum of 20,000,000, or receive 75,000 per month for 50 years at an annual rate of 2% (compounded monthly). Which option is more favorable for you?

Show solution

Monthly rate r = 0.02 / 12 ≈ 0.0016667; total number of periods n = 600

PVA = 75,000 × [1 − (1 + r)−600] / r ≈ 28,431,639.24

The present value of the installment option (approximately 28,431,639) is higher than the lump-sum amount of 20,000,000. Assuming the same discount rate and that payments are made as promised, the installment option is more favorable; however, factors such as inflation, counterparty default risk, and liquidity needs must still be considered.

Chapter review and further practice

  • An annuity is a series of cash flows of equal amount at equal intervals; first determine whether it is an ordinary annuity, an annuity due, or a perpetuity.
  • Annuity due = ordinary annuity × (1 + r), because every cash flow occurs one period earlier.
  • For monthly payment problems, always convert the annual rate to a monthly rate and the number of years to months before applying the formula.
  • Complete all six exercises above, and try verifying the answers using another method (for example, listing the cash flows period by period).
