Chapter 3: Valuation of Fixed-Income and Equity Securities

Introduction

This chapter applies the time value of money and annuity concepts to the two most common securities in financial markets: bonds and stocks. You will learn how to price these securities and understand how key factors — the market interest rate, the coupon rate, and the dividend growth rate — determine their fair value.

3.1 Bond Basics and Cash Flows

Core Concept

A bond is a debt instrument. A bondholder receives two types of cash flow: periodic interest payments (coupons) and the return of principal (face value) at maturity.

TermDefinitionSymbol
Face ValueThe amount repaid to the holder at maturityFV
Coupon RateThe annual interest rate paid (as a proportion of face value)$r_c$
Market Rate (YTM)The return required by investorsr
MaturityThe point in time the bond's life endsn

Relationship: coupon payment per period $C = FV \times \text{coupon rate}$ (i.e., $C = FV \times r_c$). Note that $C$ is an amount, while the coupon rate $r_c$ is a rate.

3.2 Bond Valuation Model

1. Pricing Formula

$$Price = C \left[ \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} \right] + FV (1+r)^{-n}$$

2. Worked Example 3.1: Pricing a Premium Bond

Scenario: Face value $FV=100{,}000$, coupon rate $r_c=4\%$ paid annually, maturing in three years ($n=3$), market rate $r=3.8\%$.

Coupon $C = FV \times r_c = 100{,}000 \times 4\% = 4{,}000$

$$Price \approx 100{,}557.13 \text{ (a premium bond)}$$

3. Premium, Discount and Par Bonds

Whether a bond is priced above or below its face value depends on the relative size of the coupon rate $r_c$ and the market rate $r$:

Bond TypeRate ConditionPrice vs. Face Value
Premium Bond$r_c > r$$Price > FV$
Par Bond$r_c = r$$Price = FV$
Discount Bond$r_c < r$$Price < FV$

Intuition: when the coupon rate exceeds the market rate, the bond pays more generous coupons than comparable investments in the market, so investors are willing to pay extra (a premium) for it. When the coupon rate is lower, the bond must be sold at a reduced price (a discount). When the two are equal, the price equals face value (par).

In Example 3.1: $r_c = 4\% > r = 3.8\%$, so the price of $100{,}557.13$ is above the face value of $100{,}000$ — a premium bond.

3.3 The Relationship Between Bond Price and Market Rate (Interest Rate Risk)

Core Concept

Once a bond is issued, its coupon $C$ and face value $FV$ are fixed, but the market rate $r$ moves with economic conditions. A bond's price is obtained by discounting these fixed cash flows at the market rate, so the two move in opposite directions:

  • Market rate rises → larger discount rate → bond price falls
  • Market rate falls → smaller discount rate → bond price rises

The risk that bond prices fluctuate because market rates change is called interest rate risk. Even if the issuer never defaults, an investor who sells before maturity can still lose money if rates have risen.

1. One Bond at Different Market Rates

Take the bond from Example 3.1 (face value $100{,}000$, coupon rate $r_c=4\%$, annual coupons, $3$ years to maturity):

Market rate $r$Bond priceBond type
$3\%$$102{,}828.61$Premium
$3.8\%$$100{,}557.13$Premium (Example 3.1)
$4\%$$100{,}000.00$Par
$5\%$$97{,}276.75$Discount
$6\%$$94{,}653.98$Discount

As the market rate goes up, the price goes down; once the market rate passes the $4\%$ coupon rate, the bond turns from a premium bond into a discount bond.

2. Worked Example 3.2: Maturity and Interest Rate Risk

Scenario: The market rate rises from $4\%$ to $5\%$. How much does the price of each of the following bonds change? All have a face value of $100{,}000$ and pay annual coupons.

BondPrice at $r=4\%$Price at $r=5\%$Price change
A: 3-year, coupon rate $4\%$$100{,}000.00$$97{,}276.75$$-2.72\%$
B: 10-year, coupon rate $4\%$$100{,}000.00$$92{,}278.27$$-7.72\%$
C: 10-year zero-coupon bond$67{,}556.42$$61{,}391.33$$-9.13\%$

Bonds A and B are priced with the formula in Section 3.2; bond C pays no coupons ($C=0$), so only the face value is discounted:

$$Price_{C} = \frac{100{,}000}{(1.05)^{10}} \approx 61{,}391.33$$

Takeaways:

  • The longer the maturity, the greater the interest rate risk: A and B have the same coupon rate, yet the 10-year bond falls almost three times as much as the 3-year bond — cash flows further in the future are discounted over more periods, so they are more sensitive to rate changes.
  • The lower the coupon rate, the greater the interest rate risk: B and C both mature in 10 years, but the zero-coupon bond's entire cash flow arrives in year 10, so it falls the most.

Note: if the market rate instead falls from $4\%$ to $3\%$, bond B rises to $108{,}530.20$ ($+8.53\%$) — a larger gain than the $-7.72\%$ loss from a 1% rate rise. Duration, the standard measure of a bond's price sensitivity to rates, is covered in later fixed-income courses.

3.4 Stock Valuation: The Dividend Discount Model and the Gordon Growth Model

Core Concept

Just like a bond, a stock is worth the present value of the future cash flows it provides. For a common shareholder, those cash flows come from two sources: the dividends received while holding the stock, and the price at which the stock is later sold.

1. Starting with a One-Year Holding Period

Suppose an investor buys the stock today, holds it for one year, receives a dividend $D_1$ at year-end and then sells at price $P_1$. With a required return of $r$, today's fair price is:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1 + P_1}{1+r}$$

But the price one year from now, $P_1$, itself depends on the year-2 dividend $D_2$ and the selling price $P_2$; $P_2$ depends on $D_3$ and $P_3$; and so on.

2. The Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

Pushing this logic forward forever, the final selling price is discounted to almost nothing, and the stock price equals the present value of all future dividends:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{1+r} + \frac{D_2}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{D_3}{(1+r)^3} + \cdots = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

The difficulty is that a stock has no maturity date and its future dividends are uncertain, so they cannot be forecast one by one. In practice we make simplifying assumptions about how dividends evolve. The two most common special cases follow.

ItemBondCommon stock

4. Special Case 1: The Zero-Growth Model (a Perpetuity)

If the dividend is a fixed amount $D$ every year and never changes ($g=0$), the dividend discount model is simply the perpetuity from Chapter 2:

$$P_0 = \frac{D}{r}$$

This model fits preferred stock, which pays a fixed dividend every year and has no maturity date.

5. Worked Example 3.3: Valuing Preferred Stock

Scenario: A preferred stock pays a fixed dividend of $D=5$ at the end of every year and has no maturity date. If investors require a return of $r=8\%$, what is its fair price?

$$P_0 = \frac{D}{r} = \frac{5}{0.08} = 62.5$$

Intuition: buy at $62.5$, collect $5$ every year, and your return is exactly $5 / 62.5 = 8\%$. This is the same logic as the scholarship foundation in Chapter 2, Example 2.4.

6. Special Case 2: Constant Growth — the Gordon Growth Model (GGM)

If dividends grow forever at a constant rate $g$, so that $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$, $D_2 = D_0(1+g)^2$, …, the dividend discount model simplifies to a growing perpetuity (the inflation-adjusted scholarship in the "Further thinking" part of Chapter 2, Example 2.4):

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r-g} = \frac{D_0(1+g)}{r-g}$$

Here $D_0$ is the dividend just paid, $D_1$ is next period's (one year from now) dividend, $r$ is the required return, and $g$ is the perpetual dividend growth rate. When using the GGM, keep in mind:

  • Constant, perpetual growth: dividends are assumed to grow at the same rate $g$ forever.
  • $r$ must exceed $g$: if $g \ge r$, the denominator $r-g \le 0$, the present value of future dividends does not converge, and the formula breaks down.
  • The numerator is $D_1$, not $D_0$: if a problem gives the dividend "just paid" or "paid this year" ($D_0$), multiply by $(1+g)$ first. This is the most common mistake.
  • Where it applies: mature companies with stable growth that pay dividends consistently.

7. Worked Example 3.4: Pricing with the Gordon Model

Scenario: A company has just paid a dividend of $D_0=2.00$. Dividends are expected to grow at $g=5\%$ a year, and investors require a return of $r=10\%$.

$D_1 = 2.00 \times 1.05 = 2.10$

$$P_0 = \frac{2.10}{0.10 - 0.05} = 42$$

8. What Makes Up the Required Return: Dividend Yield + Capital Gains Yield

Rearranging the GGM gives:

$$r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$
  • $D_1 / P_0$: the dividend yield — the return that comes from dividends.
  • $g$: the capital gains yield — the return that comes from the rising share price. Under the GGM the price also grows at rate $g$: $P_1 = P_0(1+g)$.

Check with Example 3.4: dividend yield $2.10 / 42 = 5\%$, capital gains yield $g = 5\%$, total $10\% = r$. The price one year later is $P_1 = 42 \times 1.05 = 44.10$; plugging into the one-period formula gives $(2.10 + 44.10) / 1.10 = 42$, matching the GGM result.

9. Worked Example 3.5: How the Required Return and Growth Rate Affect the Price

Scenario: Continue Example 3.4 ($D_0=2.00$).

  • If the required return rises from $10\%$ to $12\%$ ($g$ stays at $5\%$): $P_0 = \dfrac{2.10}{0.12 - 0.05} = 30$, a drop of about $28.6\%$.
  • If the growth rate rises from $5\%$ to $6\%$ ($r$ stays at $10\%$): $D_1 = 2.00 \times 1.06 = 2.12$, $P_0 = \dfrac{2.12}{0.10 - 0.06} = 53$, a rise of about $26.2\%$.

A higher required return lowers the stock price — the same logic as "a higher market rate lowers the bond price" in Section 3.3. Because the GGM denominator $r-g$ is usually small, even a small change in $r$ or $g$ moves the price a lot.

10. Supplement: Limitations of the Model

  • Companies that pay no dividends (for example, many high-growth tech stocks) cannot be valued directly with the dividend discount model.
  • During a high-growth phase, a company's growth rate may temporarily exceed $r$. A two-stage growth model is then used: discount the high-growth dividends year by year, then use the GGM for the value once growth becomes stable.
  • In practice, analysts also use relative valuation, such as price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios compared with peers.
