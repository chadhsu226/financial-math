Chapter 5: Capital Budgeting and Long-Term Investment Decisions

Introduction

Businesses constantly face "should we invest?" decisions: buy a new machine, build a factory, open a new branch, or launch a product line. The process of planning and evaluating such long-term investment projects is called capital budgeting.

Capital budgeting decisions share three features: large amounts of money, long-lasting effects, and being hard to reverse. Most importantly, the investment is paid today, while the payoffs arrive over many future years. Money at different points in time cannot simply be added up; we must use the present value ideas from Chapters 1 and 2 to bring future cash flows back to today. This is the Chapter 3 idea — an asset's value is the present value of its future cash flows — applied to a company's investment projects.

The chapter proceeds as follows:

  1. 5.1: project cash flows and the cost of capital, and how to draw a project's cash flow timeline.
  2. 5.2 Net Present Value (NPV): discount all cash flows to today to see whether the project creates value.
  3. 5.3 Internal Rate of Return (IRR): find the project's own rate of return and compare it with the cost of capital.
  4. 5.4 Payback methods: a supporting view based on "how long until we get our money back".
  5. 5.5 Conflicts between NPV and IRR: when the two rules disagree, which one should we follow?

5.1 Project Cash Flows and the Cost of Capital

1. A Project's Cash Flows $CF_t$

The first step in evaluating a project is to list the cash flow (CF) it generates in each period. $CF_t$ denotes the net cash flow at the end of period $t$:

TimeCash flowWhat it includesSign
$t=0$ (today)Initial investment $CF_0$Buying equipment, fit-out, up-front costsOutflow, negative
$t=1, 2, \ldots, n$Operating net cash flowsThe project's additional cash revenue minus its additional cash costsUsually positive
$t=n$ (end)Salvage valueProceeds from selling the equipment when the project ends (if any)Inflow, positive

The sign convention matches Chapter 2: from the investor's (the company's) point of view, inflows are positive and outflows are negative. Unless stated otherwise, operating cash flows are assumed to occur at the end of each period.

2. The Cost of Capital $r$

The cost of capital is the minimum return a company requires from a project, and it is the discount rate $r$ used to discount the project's future cash flows. It is an opportunity cost: the return the company could earn by investing the same money in other opportunities of similar risk instead. If a project cannot even earn its cost of capital, the money is better used elsewhere.

3. The Example 5.1 Scenario: Should the Bakery Buy a New Oven?

Scenario: Lin runs a bakery and is thinking about spending $100{,}000$ on a large oven. With the new oven he can bake one more batch of bread a day; after the extra costs of flour, butter, electricity and packaging, he expects an additional net cash inflow of $40{,}000$ at the end of each year. The oven lasts $3$ years and has no salvage value.

If Lin does not buy the oven, he can invest the money elsewhere at similar risk and earn $10\%$ a year, so he sets the cost of capital at $r=10\%$.

As cash flows: $CF_0 = -100{,}000$, $CF_1 = CF_2 = CF_3 = 40{,}000$. The timeline is:

Simply adding them up, the three inflows total $120{,}000$ — $20{,}000$ more than the $100{,}000$ invested, which looks profitable. But the three amounts arrive at different times and cannot be added directly; the next section uses NPV to bring them back to today first.

5.2 Net Present Value (NPV)

Core Concept

Net present value (NPV) discounts all of a project's cash flows (including the initial investment) to today at the cost of capital $r$ and adds them up. It measures how much value the project adds to the company today:

$$NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} = CF_0 + \frac{CF_1}{1+r} + \frac{CF_2}{(1+r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{CF_n}{(1+r)^n}$$

Because $CF_0$ is the (negative) initial investment, NPV can also be read as "the present value of future inflows" minus "the initial investment". NPV is considered the gold standard of capital budgeting because it directly measures the wealth a project creates for shareholders.

1. NPV Decision Rule

  • If $NPV > 0$: the project earns more than its cost of capital; accept it — it increases shareholder wealth.
  • If $NPV < 0$: the project earns less than its cost of capital; reject it.
  • If $NPV = 0$: the project earns exactly its cost of capital; accepting or rejecting makes no difference.

2. Worked Example 5.1: NPV of the Bakery's New Oven

Continuing the scenario in Section 5.1, discount each cash flow at $r=10\%$:

Year $t$Cash flow $CF_t$Discount factor $1/(1.10)^t$Present value
$0$$-100{,}000$$1.0000$$-100{,}000.00$
$1$$40{,}000$$0.9091$$36{,}363.64$
$2$$40{,}000$$0.8264$$33{,}057.85$
$3$$40{,}000$$0.7513$$30{,}052.59$
Total (NPV)$-525.92$
$$NPV = -100{,}000 + \frac{40{,}000}{1.10} + \frac{40{,}000}{1.10^2} + \frac{40{,}000}{1.10^3} \approx -525.92$$

Annuity shortcut: the three inflows are equal, so they form an ordinary annuity from Chapter 2 and we can use the annuity present value formula directly:

$$NPV = -100{,}000 + 40{,}000 \times \left[\frac{1 - (1.10)^{-3}}{0.10}\right] = -100{,}000 + 99{,}474.08 \approx -525.92$$

Decision: $NPV < 0$, so buying is not recommended. The inflows total $120{,}000$, but in today's money they are worth only $99{,}474.08$ — less than the oven's $100{,}000$ price. In other words, the oven earns slightly less than the $10\%$ Lin requires; he is better off putting the money into another $10\%$ opportunity.

Think about it: if the annual net inflow were $41{,}000$ instead, NPV would be $41{,}000 \times 2.4869 - 100{,}000 \approx 1{,}960.93$ and the decision would flip to "buy". NPV is sensitive to the cash flow forecasts, which is why practitioners estimate cash flows carefully.

5.3 Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

Core Concept

NPV tells us "how much money" a project makes, but many managers prefer to ask, "What is this investment's rate of return?" The internal rate of return (IRR) is the project's own annual return, defined as the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero:

$$\sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t} = 0$$

"Internal" means the IRR depends only on the project's own cash flows, not on the external cost of capital. You can also think of the IRR as the project's break-even discount rate: the project is worthwhile when the cost of capital is below the IRR, and not when it is above.

1. Decision Rule

  • If $IRR > r$ (cost of capital): the project's return beats the requirement; accept it.
  • If $IRR < r$ (cost of capital): the project's return falls short; reject it.

2. The NPV Profile: Where Is the IRR?

Using the oven from Example 5.1, compute NPV at different discount rates:

Discount rate $r$$0\%$$5\%$$8\%$$9\%$$10\%$$12\%$$15\%$
NPV$20{,}000.00$$8{,}929.92$$3{,}083.88$$1{,}251.79$$-525.92$$-3{,}926.75$$-8{,}671.00$

The higher the discount rate, the smaller the present value of future cash flows and the lower the NPV. Connecting these points gives the NPV profile; where it crosses the horizontal axis ($NPV=0$) is the IRR. At $r=0\%$ the NPV is simply the undiscounted net inflow, $120{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 20{,}000$.

3. Worked Example 5.2: Estimating IRR by Trial and Error and Interpolation

The IRR equation usually cannot be solved directly. We use trial and error to find where NPV turns from positive to negative, then estimate by linear interpolation:

  1. Try $r=9\%$: $NPV = 40{,}000 \times \left[\dfrac{1-(1.09)^{-3}}{0.09}\right] - 100{,}000 \approx 1{,}251.79 > 0$, so the IRR is above $9\%$.
  2. Try $r=10\%$: $NPV \approx -525.92 < 0$, so the IRR is below $10\%$. The IRR therefore lies between $9\%$ and $10\%$.
  3. Interpolate: NPV falls from $1{,}251.79$ to $-525.92$, a drop of $1{,}777.71$; reaching $0$ requires $1{,}251.79$ of that drop:
$$IRR \approx 9\% + \frac{1{,}251.79}{1{,}251.79 - (-525.92)} \times (10\% - 9\%) \approx 9\% + 0.70\% = 9.70\%$$

The exact value from Excel's IRR function or a financial calculator is about $9.701\%$, almost identical to the interpolated estimate. Interpolation approximates the curved NPV profile with a straight line; the closer the two trial rates, the more accurate the estimate.

Intuition for IRR: imagine Lin deposits $100{,}000$ in an account paying $9.70\%$ a year and withdraws $40{,}000$ at the end of each year:

YearOpening balanceInterest ($9.70\%$)WithdrawalClosing balance
$1$$100{,}000.00$$9{,}701.03$$40{,}000$$69{,}701.03$
$2$$69{,}701.03$$6{,}761.71$$40{,}000$$36{,}462.74$
$3$$36{,}462.74$$3{,}537.26$$40{,}000$$0.00$

After three years the account is exactly empty. So the oven is equivalent to an investment earning $9.70\%$ a year.

Decision: $IRR \approx 9.70\% < r = 10\%$, so buying is not recommended — the same conclusion as NPV.

4. How NPV and IRR Relate

  • For a conventional project (invest first, receive later), the NPV profile slopes downward, so $IRR > r$ and $NPV > 0$ are the same statement; the two rules give the same accept/reject decision for a single project.
  • When comparing mutually exclusive projects (only one can be chosen), the two rules can disagree, and a project whose cash flows change sign more than once can have several IRRs. These cases are discussed in Section 5.5.

5.4 Supplementary Methods: Payback Period

1. Payback Period

  • Measures how long it takes to recover the initial investment.
  • Drawback: it ignores the time value of money.

2. Discounted Payback Period

  • Accounts for the time value of money by measuring how long it takes to recover the discounted value of the investment.

3. Worked Example 5.3: Calculating the Payback Period

Scenario: An initial investment of $250{,}000$, cost of capital $r=12\%$, with cash inflows over the next four years of $100{,}000$, $120{,}000$, $80{,}000$, and $50{,}000$.

$$Payback\ Period = 2 + \frac{30{,}000}{80{,}000} = 2.375 \text{ years}$$
$$Discounted\ Payback\ Period \approx 3.255 \text{ years}$$

5.5 Conflicts Between NPV and IRR

For a single project, NPV and IRR reach the same conclusion. But when choosing among mutually exclusive projects (only one can be chosen), the two rules can disagree. Common causes are differences in project scale or in the timing of cash flows.

Worked Example 5.4: An NPV vs. IRR Conflict

Scenario: Cost of capital $r=10\%$, two mutually exclusive projects:

ProjectInitial InvestmentYear 1 InflowIRRNPV
S (Small)$-100$$120$$20.00\%$$9.09$
L (Large)$-1{,}000$$1{,}150$$15.00\%$$45.45$
Final decision: choose Project L. Even though Project S has a higher IRR, Project L generates a larger absolute NPV, which creates more wealth for shareholders.
Decision priority: NPV takes precedence.
