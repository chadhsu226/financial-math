4.1 Determining the Loan Payment

The total loan principal (PV) is, in essence, the present value today of a series of future level payments (an ordinary annuity C). The ordinary annuity present-value formula can therefore be solved for the payment per period, C.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 4

Loan Payment Formula

C = PV × r / [1 − (1 + r)−n]

This follows from solving the ordinary annuity present-value formula PV = C × [1−(1+r)−n]/r for C.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 4

Example 4.1: Calculating a Mortgage Payment

A mortgage of 3,000,000, annual rate 2.4%, compounded monthly, repaid over 20 years (240 months). How much must be repaid each month?

Periodic rate r = 2.4% / 12 = 0.002, total periods n = 240

C = 3,000,000 × 0.002 / [1 − (1.002)−240]
C ≈ 15,751.34

4.2 The Amortization Schedule

The amortization schedule records in detail how each payment is split between interest and principal. Under level payments, as the principal balance falls, interest declines each period while the principal portion rises.

  • Interest for the period: Interestt = beginning balance × r
  • Principal repaid: Principalt = C − Interestt
  • Ending balance: Ending balancet = beginning balancet − Principalt

4.3 Calculating the Outstanding Loan Balance

The outstanding balance after period k equals the present value of all remaining (n − k) payments:

OLBk = C × [1 − (1 + r)−(n−k)] / r

This is the present-value (prospective) method: it discounts the remaining future payments back to the present at period k.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 4

Example 4.3: Calculating the Remaining Balance

Continuing Example 4.1, what is the remaining loan balance after 5 years of payments (k = 60)?

OLB60 = 15,751.34 × [1 − (1.002)−180] / 0.002
OLB60 ≈ 2,379,025.69

Although 5 years of payments have been made, only about 620,974.31 of principal has been repaid — illustrating how most of a loan's early payments go toward interest.

Comprehensive Exercise 1

A corporate loan of 1,500,000, annual rate 4.8%, compounded monthly, repaid over 5 years. What is the monthly payment? What are the interest and principal in the first period?

Show solution and calculation

r = 4.8%/12 = 0.004, N = 60

C = 1,500,000 × 0.004 / [1 − (1.004)−60] ≈ 28,169.61

First-period interest = 1,500,000 × 0.004 = 6,000.00

First-period principal = 28,169.61 − 6,000.00 = 22,169.61

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 4

Comprehensive Exercise 3

Explain how the amortization schedule works: why does the interest portion of a level payment decline over time?

Show solution and calculation

Because each period's interest is calculated as the beginning balance × r, and each payment first covers that period's interest, with the remainder applied to principal. Since the outstanding principal falls with every payment, the base on which interest is calculated (the beginning balance) also shrinks each period. With the total payment held fixed, the interest share therefore declines period by period while the principal share rises.

Comprehensive Exercise 4

A loan of 5,000,000 for a 25-year term at an annual rate of 3%, repaid monthly. How much total interest has been paid after 10 years?

Show solution and calculation

r = 3%/12 = 0.0025, N = 300, k = 120

C = 5,000,000 × 0.0025 / [1 − (1.0025)−300] ≈ 23,710.57

Total payments over 10 years = 23,710.57 × 120 ≈ 2,845,267.88

OLB120 ≈ 3,433,419.64, principal repaid ≈ 5,000,000 − 3,433,419.64 = 1,566,580.36

Total interest ≈ 2,845,267.88 − 1,566,580.36 ≈ 1,278,687.53

Chapter review and further practice

  • The loan payment C is found by solving the annuity present-value formula so that the present value of all future level payments equals the loan principal.
  • The amortization schedule shows how each payment splits into interest and principal: interest declines with the falling balance while principal rises, though the total payment stays constant.
  • The outstanding balance OLB can be viewed as the present value of an annuity over the remaining periods, used to determine the amount due at any point for prepayment or refinancing.
  • Complete all four exercises above, and try building your own amortization schedule to verify the trends in interest and principal.
