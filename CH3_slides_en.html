3.1 Fundamentals of bonds and their cash flows

A bond is a debt instrument. Bondholders receive two types of cash flows: periodic interest payments (coupons) and the return of principal (face value) at maturity.

3.2 The bond pricing model

Price = C × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r + FV × (1 + r)−n

The first term is the present value of the coupon annuity, and the second term is the present value of the face value at maturity; their sum is the bond's fair price.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.1: Pricing a premium bond

Face value FV = 100,000, coupon rate rc = 4%, paid annually, maturing in three years (n = 3), with a market rate of r = 3.8%.

Coupon C = FV × rc = 100,000 × 4% = 4,000

Price = 4,000 × [1 − (1.038)−3] / 0.038 + 100,000 × (1.038)−3
Price ≈ 100,557.13 (premium bond)

Because the coupon rate (4%) exceeds the market rate (3.8%), investors are willing to pay a price above face value.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

When the coupon rate exceeds the market rate, the coupons beat comparable investments, so investors pay more than face value.

3.3 Bond prices and market interest rates (interest rate risk)

Coupons and face value are fixed at issue; the bond price discounts them at the market rate:

  • Market rate rises → larger discount rate → bond price falls
  • Market rate falls → smaller discount rate → bond price rises

The risk that bond prices move because market rates change is interest rate risk. Even without default, selling before maturity can mean a loss.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.2: Takeaways

  • Longer maturity, more interest rate risk: A and B have the same coupon rate, but the 10-year bond falls almost three times as much.
  • Lower coupon rate, more interest rate risk: among 10-year bonds, the zero-coupon bond — all cash at maturity — falls the most.

If rates instead fall from 4% to 3%, bond B gains 8.53%, more than the 7.72% it loses when rates rise 1%. Duration, the formal measure of rate sensitivity, comes in later courses.

3.4 Stock valuation: a stock's cash flows

Stock price = PV of future cash flows. A shareholder receives dividends while holding the stock and the price when selling.

Hold for one year: receive dividend D₁ at year-end, then sell at P₁:

P₀ = (D₁ + P₁) / (1 + r)

But P₁ depends on D₂ and P₂, P₂ on D₃ and P₃, and so on.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

The dividend discount model (DDM)

Pushing forward forever, the final selling price is discounted to almost nothing, so the price equals the PV of all future dividends:

P₀ = D₁/(1+r) + D₂/(1+r)2 + D₃/(1+r)3 + ⋯

A stock has no maturity and uncertain dividends, so we simplify how dividends evolve: zero growth or constant growth.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Special case 1: zero growth (a perpetuity)

If the dividend is a fixed D every year (g = 0), the dividend discount model is the Chapter 2 perpetuity:

P₀ = D / r

This fits preferred stock, which pays a fixed dividend every year and never matures.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.3: Valuing preferred stock

A preferred stock pays a fixed dividend D = 5 at the end of every year and never matures. The required return is r = 8%. What is its fair price?

P₀ = 5 / 0.08 = 62.5

Buy at 62.5, collect 5 a year, and your return is exactly 5 / 62.5 = 8% — the same logic as the scholarship foundation in Chapter 2, Example 2.4.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Special case 2: constant growth — the GGM

Dividends grow forever at a constant rate g, a growing perpetuity:

P₀ = D₁ / (r − g) = D₀(1 + g) / (r − g)
  • Dividends grow at a constant g forever.
  • r must exceed g, or the formula breaks down.
  • The numerator is D₁: if given the dividend just paid, D₀, multiply by (1 + g) first.
FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.4: Pricing with the Gordon Growth Model

A company has just paid D₀ = 2.00; g = 5%, r = 10%.

D₁ = 2.00 × 1.05 = 2.10
P₀ = 2.10 / (0.10 − 0.05) = 42
FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

What makes up the required return

Rearrange the GGM:

r = D₁ / P₀ + g
  • D₁ / P₀: dividend yield — the return from dividends.
  • g: capital gains yield — under the GGM the price also grows at g, P₁ = P₀(1 + g).

Example 3.4: 2.10 / 42 = 5%, plus g = 5%, totals 10% = r. P₁ = 42 × 1.05 = 44.10, and (2.10 + 44.10) / 1.10 = 42.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.5: How r and g affect the price

Continue Example 3.4 (D₀ = 2.00):

CaseCalculationPrice
Base: r = 10%, g = 5%2.10 / 0.0542
r rises to 12%2.10 / 0.0730 (−28.6%)
g rises to 6%2.12 / 0.0453 (+26.2%)

A higher required return lowers the price — the same logic as interest rate risk for bonds. Because r − g is small, small changes in r or g move the price a lot.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Supplement: limitations of the model

  • Companies that pay no dividends (for example, many high-growth tech stocks) cannot be valued directly with the dividend discount model.
  • In a high-growth phase g may temporarily exceed r; use a two-stage growth model: discount the high-growth dividends year by year, then apply the GGM once growth stabilizes.
  • Practitioners also use relative valuation, such as P/E ratios compared with peers.

Comprehensive Exercise 5

All else equal, is interest rate risk greatest for a long-term bond, a short-term bond, or a zero-coupon bond? Why?

Show solution and calculation

A long-term zero-coupon bond.

  • The longer the maturity, the more periods each cash flow is discounted over, so the more sensitive the price.
  • A zero-coupon bond receives everything at maturity, with no early coupons, so it is more sensitive than a coupon bond of the same maturity (Example 3.2: −9.13% vs −7.72%).

Comprehensive Exercise 9

What are the main assumptions of the GGM? If the dividend growth rate exceeds the required return, can the GGM be used?

Show solution and calculation
  • Dividends grow at a constant rate g forever.
  • The required return r must exceed g for the present value to converge.
  • The company keeps operating and paying dividends indefinitely.

If g ≥ r, the denominator r − g ≤ 0 and the GGM does not apply; use a two-stage growth model or another method.

Chapter review and further practice

  • Bond price = PV of the coupon annuity + PV of the face value; the coupon rate versus the market rate decides premium, discount or par.
  • Market rates and bond prices move inversely (interest rate risk); longer maturity and lower coupons mean more risk.
  • Stock price = PV of all future dividends; use P₀ = D/r for zero growth (preferred stock) and the GGM P₀ = D₁/(r−g), with r > g, for constant growth.
  • Required return = dividend yield + capital gains yield (r = D₁/P₀ + g). Work through all nine exercises and check your answers.
