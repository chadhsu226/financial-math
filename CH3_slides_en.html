3.1 Fundamentals of bonds and their cash flows

A bond is a debt instrument. Bondholders receive two types of cash flows: periodic interest payments (coupons) and the return of principal (face value) at maturity.

Coupon RateThe interest rate paid each yearC
Market Rate (YTM)The rate of return investors requirer
MaturityThe point at which the bond's life endsn

3.2 The bond pricing model

Price = C × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r + FV × (1 + r)−n

The first term is the present value of the coupon annuity, and the second term is the present value of the face value at maturity; their sum is the bond's fair price.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.1: Pricing a premium bond

Face value FV = 100,000, coupon rate 4%, paid annually, maturing in three years (n = 3), with a market rate of r = 3.8%.

Coupon C = 100,000 × 4% = 4,000

Price = 4,000 × [1 − (1.038)−3] / 0.038 + 100,000 × (1.038)−3
Price ≈ 100,557.13 (premium bond)

Because the coupon rate (4%) exceeds the market rate (3.8%), investors are willing to pay a price above face value.

3.3 The relationship between bond prices and market interest rates (interest rate risk)

An inverse relationship exists between a bond's price and market interest rates:

  • Market rates rise → bond prices fall
  • Market rates fall → bond prices rise
ComparisonBond status
Coupon rate > market ratePremium bond (Price > FV)
Coupon rate = market ratePar bond (Price = FV)
Coupon rate < market rateDiscount bond (Price < FV)

3.4 Equity valuation and the Gordon Growth Model (GGM)

The Gordon Growth Model (GGM) assumes that dividends grow perpetually at a constant rate (g), and discounts this infinite stream of growing cash flows back to its present value.

The model holds only when r > g; otherwise the present value diverges and the formula no longer applies.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

The GGM pricing formula

P₀ = D₁ / (r − g)

where D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g); r is the required rate of return; and g is the perpetual dividend growth rate.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Example 3.2: Pricing with the Gordon Growth Model

D₀ = 2.00, g = 5%, r = 10%.

D₁ = 2.00 × 1.05 = 2.10
P₀ = 2.10 / (0.10 − 0.05) = 42

Comprehensive Exercise 4

When the coupon rate exceeds the market rate, what happens to the bond price?

Show solution and calculation

Premium (Price > face value)

Because investors receive a coupon each period that exceeds the return the market requires, they are willing to pay a price above face value, until the overall return is brought back down to the market rate.

FINANCIAL MATHEMATICS / CHAPTER 3

Comprehensive Exercise 5

What are the main assumptions of the Gordon Growth Model?

Show solution and calculation
  • Dividends grow perpetually at a constant rate g each period, without interruption or a change in the growth rate.
  • The required return r must exceed the growth rate g (r > g) for the present value to converge.
  • The company continues operating and paying dividends indefinitely.

Chapter review and further practice

  • Bond price = present value of the coupon annuity + present value of the face value; the relative size of the coupon rate and the market rate determines whether the bond trades at a premium, a discount, or at par.
  • Market rates and bond prices move inversely — this is interest rate risk.
  • The Gordon Growth Model discounts a perpetually growing dividend stream into the simple formula P₀=D₁/(r−g), which holds only when r>g.
  • Complete all five exercises above, and try verifying the answers using another method (for example, discounting each period's cash flow individually).
