Chapter 1: Time Value of Money and Interest Fundamentals

Introduction

Welcome to the core of financial mathematics! This chapter walks you through the starting point of every financial decision: the time value of money (TVM).

You will learn how to quantify the value of money at different points in time — the foundation for evaluating any investment, computing a loan, or planning for retirement. We will work systematically through simple and compound interest to understand how money grows, and master the key tools of present value (PV), future value (FV), and the rate used to compare offers on a like-for-like basis (the effective rate).

1.1 Simple Interest: Linear Growth

Core Concept

Simple interest is the most basic way to calculate interest. Its defining feature is that interest is computed only on the original principal ($P$). Growth under simple interest is linear, and it is typically used for short-term loans or specific notes.

Simple Interest Formulas

Total interest ($I$):

$$I = P \times r \times n$$

Future value ($FV$):

$$FV = P + I = P(1+r \times n)$$

Notation

  • $P$: Principal — the original amount invested or borrowed.
  • $r$: Annual interest rate, expressed as a decimal.
  • $n$: Number of periods, usually the total number of years.

1.2 Compound Interest and Future Value: Exponential Growth

Core Concept

Compound interest is the standard convention in modern financial markets. Its core idea is "interest on interest": the interest earned each period is added back to the principal, and the next period's interest is computed on this new, larger base. Growth under compounding is exponential, and understanding it is the key to understanding long-term investment returns.

1. Compounding Once a Year

$$FV = P(1+r)^n$$

2. Compounding Frequency

When compounding occurs $m$ times per year:

$$FV = P\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mn}$$

3. Continuous Compounding

$$FV = P e^{rn}$$

Worked Example 1.1: The Effect of Compounding Frequency

Suppose an investment has a principal of $P=8,000$, an annual rate of $r=5\%=0.05$, and a term of $n=4$ years.

Annual compounding ($m=1$):

$$FV = 8000 \times (1+0.05)^4 \approx 9{,}724.05$$

Monthly compounding ($m=12$):

$$FV = 8000 \times \left(1+\frac{0.05}{12}\right)^{48} \approx 9{,}767.24$$

Continuous compounding:

$$FV = 8000 \times e^{0.05 \times 4} \approx 9{,}771.22$$

1.3 Present Value and Discounting

Core Concept

Present value (PV) is the reverse application of the time value of money. It answers the question: "How much do I need to invest today to have a certain amount ($FV$) in the future?" Converting a future value back into a present value is called discounting.

1. Present Value Formula

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^n}$$

2. Worked Example 1.2: Present Value Calculation

Suppose you want to have $FV=15{,}000$ in $10$ years, and the discount rate is $r=5\%$. How much do you need to deposit today?

$$PV = \frac{15000}{(1+0.05)^{10}} \approx 9{,}208.70$$

1.4 Comparing Rates: Nominal vs. Effective Rate

Core Concept

  • Nominal rate ($r_{Nominal}$): The annual rate stated in a contract, without accounting for compounding frequency.
  • Effective Annual Rate (EAR): The actual annual rate earned or paid once compounding frequency ($m$) is taken into account.

1. Effective Annual Rate (EAR) Formula

$$EAR = \left(1+\frac{r_{Nominal}}{m}\right)^m - 1$$

2. Worked Example 1.3: Calculating the Effective Rate

Suppose the nominal annual rate $r_{Nominal}$ is $12\%$, compounded monthly ($m=12$). The effective annual rate (EAR) is approximately $12.68\%$.
